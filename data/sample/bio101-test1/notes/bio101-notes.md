# BIO101 — Cells and Enzymes: Lecture Notes (Weeks 1–3)

## 1. Movement of substances

### Diffusion
Diffusion is the net movement of particles from an area of high concentration
to an area of low concentration, down a concentration gradient. It is a passive
process: it needs no energy from the cell. Small molecules such as oxygen and
carbon dioxide diffuse freely across cell membranes. For example, oxygen
diffuses from the air in the alveoli, where its concentration is high, into
the blood, where its concentration is lower.

### Facilitated diffusion
Some larger or charged particles cannot pass directly through the membrane.
They diffuse down their concentration gradient through special channel or
carrier proteins. Like diffusion, facilitated diffusion needs no energy.

### Osmosis
Osmosis is the movement of water molecules across a partially permeable
membrane from an area of high water concentration to an area of low water
concentration (down a water potential gradient). The membrane lets water
through but blocks most dissolved substances. If you place a raisin in pure
water, water moves into the raisin by osmosis and it swells. Plant root hair
cells absorb water from the soil by osmosis.

### Active transport
Active transport moves substances from an area of low concentration to an area
of high concentration, against the concentration gradient. Unlike diffusion,
it requires energy, which the cell supplies as ATP from respiration. Root hair
cells use active transport to take up mineral ions from very dilute soil water.

## 2. Enzymes

Enzymes are proteins that act as biological catalysts: they speed up reactions
without being used up, so each enzyme molecule can be reused many times. Every
enzyme has an active site whose shape fits its substrate.

### The induced-fit model
In the induced-fit model, the substrate binds to the active site and the
active site changes shape slightly, moulding itself closely around the
substrate. This strains the substrate's bonds, or holds two substrates
together in exactly the right position, so the reaction happens far more
easily. The products are then released and the enzyme is unchanged. (An older
idea, the lock-and-key model, treated the active site as a rigid lock; we now
know it flexes — hence "induced fit".)

### Effect of temperature
Raising the temperature gives molecules more kinetic energy, so there are more
frequent successful collisions and the reaction rate rises — up to an optimum.
Above the optimum, the extra energy breaks the weak bonds that hold the
enzyme's tertiary structure together. The active site loses its shape and the
enzyme is said to be denatured. The substrate no longer fits, so the rate of
an enzyme-controlled reaction falls sharply at high temperatures. Most human
enzymes denature above about 40 °C.

### Effect of pH
Each enzyme also has an optimum pH. Strongly acidic or alkaline conditions
disrupt the bonds in the enzyme and denature it. Pepsin works best around
pH 2 in the stomach, while amylase prefers near-neutral conditions in the
mouth and small intestine.

## 3. Plant and animal cells

Both plant and animal cells have a cell membrane, cytoplasm, a nucleus
containing DNA, and mitochondria, where aerobic respiration releases energy.

Plant cells have three structures that animal cells do not:

- A cell wall made of cellulose, which supports and strengthens the cell.
- Chloroplasts, which contain chlorophyll and trap light energy for
  photosynthesis.
- A large permanent vacuole filled with cell sap, which keeps the cell turgid.
  Animal cells have only small, temporary vacuoles, if any.

Because of the wall and vacuole, plant cells are usually regular in shape,
while animal cells vary. Do not confuse the cell wall with the cell membrane:
both cell types have a membrane, and only plants have the wall as well.

## 4. Blood and transport

Blood carries oxygen, nutrients, hormones and waste around the body. It has
four main parts: red blood cells, white blood cells, platelets and plasma.

### Red blood cells
Mature red blood cells have no nucleus. Losing the nucleus leaves more room
for haemoglobin, the red pigment that binds oxygen, so each cell can carry
more oxygen. Red blood cells are also biconcave discs: this shape gives a
large surface area for absorbing oxygen and lets the small, flexible cells
squeeze through narrow capillaries. They cannot divide or repair themselves,
so they are replaced roughly every 120 days.

### White blood cells, platelets and plasma
White blood cells defend against disease: some engulf pathogens
(phagocytosis), others make antibodies. Platelets are cell fragments that
help blood clot at a wound. Plasma is the pale liquid that carries dissolved
glucose, amino acids, hormones, urea and plasma proteins.
